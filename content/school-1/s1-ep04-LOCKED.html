School 1: The abandoned girl  ·  Episode 4

The mother wound.

A teaching on the first vessel of love a daughter knows, and the God who mothers her when that vessel is empty.

Welcome

Darling, we have walked through three teachings together, and each one has laid a stone beneath the woman you are becoming. The promise that the Lord receives us when our parents do not. The truth of who we are when no one is watching. The Father who has been running toward us since before we knew we were lost.

Today we walk into the fourth stone, and this one will ask something of you that the others did not. Because the mother wound is, in many ways, the quietest wound a woman carries, and the one she has been most discouraged from naming. Culture allows a daughter to grieve a father who failed her. Culture rarely permits a daughter to grieve a mother who did the same. Mothers are sacred in every culture on earth, and rightly so, because the work of mothering is one of the holiest works a woman ever does in this life. But the sacredness of the role does not make every woman who entered it equally capable of fulfilling it.

And so the daughter who carries this wound has often carried it alone, in silence, with no permission to speak it, with the constant guilt of even noticing it. Today we are going to name it. Not to dishonor anyone. Not to assign blame. To begin to heal something that has been quietly running underneath your relationship with yourself, with other women, with your own body, with your own children and with God Himself, for far longer than you have been willing to admit.

"Can a mother forget the baby at her breast and have no compassion on the child she has borne? Though she may forget, I will not forget you!"

Isaiah 49:15 NIV

This is the verse we will build the entire teaching on. Notice what God is doing in it. He is acknowledging, plainly and without softening, that mothers do sometimes forget. He does not deny it. He does not minimize it. He names it as a real possibility, and then He uses that very possibility as the canvas against which He paints His own faithfulness. Even if she forgets, I will not. The God of the universe has positioned Himself as the One who picks up exactly where the mother could not, and He has spoken it directly into His word so that no daughter would ever have to wonder whether her ache had been seen by Him.

Part 1

What the mother wound actually is.

The mother is the first vessel of love a daughter knows. She is the first heartbeat we ever hear, while we are still inside her body, and she is the first face we ever search for in this world the moment we are placed outside of it. Before we have language, before we have memory, before we have any way of understanding what is happening to us, the mother is teaching us, through her presence or her absence, through her warmth or her coldness, through her attentiveness or her distraction, what love is going to feel like for the rest of our lives.

This is not poetic exaggeration. This is structural reality. The infant brain forms its earliest pathways of attachment in the first months and years of life, and the woman who is doing the attaching is teaching the brain what to expect from love going forward. If she was warm and responsive and present, the brain learns that love is reliable. If she was cold or anxious or absent or unpredictable, the brain learns that love is something to chase, to manage, to never quite trust.

This is the mother wound at its root. Not a single dramatic event, in most cases, but a pattern that ran for years and years through a daughter's earliest formation, teaching her something about love that was not true about love at all, and that she has been operating by ever since without even knowing she was operating by it.

The daughter who did not receive sufficient mothering grows up with a hunger she cannot name. She mistakes that hunger for many things across her life. She mistakes it for romantic longing, and she pours it into men. She mistakes it for spiritual longing, and she pours it into ministry. She mistakes it for ambition, and she pours it into work. She mistakes it for hospitality, and she pours it into mothering everyone around her, sometimes at the expense of being mothered herself. The hunger is real. The targets she has been giving it to have not been able to satisfy it, because the original source of the hunger was a vessel that was empty when she first reached for it.

And so the deepest wound underneath the mother wound is not the specific failures of any specific woman. The deepest wound is that the daughter learned, before she had words, that love is something she has to earn, manage, or do without. And that learning has been quietly directing the choices of her whole life ever since.

Part 2

The faces of the wound.

The mother wound, like the father wound, comes in many shapes, and the shape matters, because the healing has to meet the specific shape of the wound and not a generic version of it.

Some mothers were physically absent. Through death, through addiction, through their own mothers having abandoned them and so leaving them with no template for staying, through circumstance, through their own broken inheritance. The daughter grew up with a literal hole where a mother was supposed to be, and she has been quietly mothering herself ever since, doing what no child was ever designed to do for herself.

Other mothers were physically present but emotionally unavailable. They cooked the meals. They washed the clothes. They did the visible work of mothering. But they were not there with the daughter in the way the daughter needed them to be there. The daughter has the strange experience of having had a mother who was always around and still feeling, somehow, deeply alone in her own house.

Other mothers were so wounded themselves that the daughter became the mother of the relationship. The daughter learned, very young, that she was responsible for managing her mother's emotions, for protecting her mother from upset, for being the strong one because her mother was too fragile to be the strong one. The role-reversal happened so early the daughter does not even remember a time when she was not the one in charge of holding her own mother up.

Other mothers competed with their daughters. Sometimes for the attention of the father. Sometimes for the spotlight in the family. Sometimes for the title of the most beautiful woman in the room. The daughter learned that her own emergence as a woman was a threat to her mother, and so she shrank to protect a relationship she should have been free to grow inside.

Other mothers controlled. They loved, in their way, but the love had conditions, and the conditions required the daughter to be a particular kind of woman to remain inside the love. The daughter spent her life performing for a love that should have been unconditional, and she has been performing for everyone in her life that way ever since.

Other mothers were critical. Of the daughter's body, of her choices, of her friends, of her dreams. Nothing was ever quite right. The daughter learned that love and criticism arrived together, and she has been confusing the two ever since, sometimes attaching herself to critical men because the critic-voice felt like home.

Each of these shapes the same wound at the root. She did not mother me the way I needed. She did not give me what only a mother can give. And so I have been operating in this world with a hunger I have not been able to satisfy through any human relationship, and I have not yet understood why.

The inversion

The God who mothers.

Now here, dear woman, the principle that has to settle in you before we go further.

The same God who has named Himself Father to the fatherless has also taken on mother imagery for the daughter whose mother could not hold her.

This is one of the most overlooked truths in the entire Word of God. He is most often called Father, and rightly so, because the Father imagery teaches us about His authority, His provision, His protection and His covenant with His children. But the Word of God does not stop at father imagery for God. The same Word that calls Him Father also gives Him mother language, deliberately and tenderly, in the very places where mother love is what a wounded daughter most needs to receive.

"As a mother comforts her child, so will I comfort you; and you will be comforted over Jerusalem."

Isaiah 66:13 NIV

God Himself, through the mouth of the prophet Isaiah, compares His own comfort to the comfort of a mother. He is not saying He is a mother. He is saying that when comfort is what His children most need, the love He gives is the love a mother gives. The deepest kind of comfort. The kind that does not solve the problem so much as it absorbs the pain. The kind that holds a daughter while she weeps and does not require her to stop weeping in order to be loved.

And in Isaiah 49, the verse we began this teaching with, He goes further still. He acknowledges that a human mother may forget, and then He declares Himself the mother who never will. Even if she forgets you, I will not forget you. The very imagery a wounded daughter needs is the imagery God Himself reaches for to describe His own faithfulness to her.

"How often I have longed to gather your children together, as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, and you were not willing."

Matthew 23:37 NIV

This is Christ Himself speaking, standing over the city of Jerusalem, weeping. And the imagery He reaches for to describe His longing for His people is the imagery of a mother hen gathering her chicks. Not a king summoning his subjects. Not a general organizing his army. A mother hen, low to the ground, calling her babies in under the warmth of her own body to keep them safe.

The God you have been afraid of, because of what your mother could not be, has been holding mother love for you the whole time. The hunger your mother could not feed, He has been waiting to feed. The arms you did not have, He has been keeping open. The gathering you did not receive at home, He has been calling you into since before you knew you were missing it.

The principle

What your mother could not give you, God Himself has been waiting to give you in her place. He has named Himself the mother who never forgets, and He has never failed to be what He has named Himself.

Part 3

The other women God positions.

There is something else God does for the daughter without sufficient mothering, and we have to see it clearly because it has been happening in your life and you may not have recognized it for what it was.

He sends other women to mother you.

Not to replace your mother. Replacement is not the way of God. He restores. He fills. He sends specific women across a wounded daughter's life to give her, in pieces and in seasons, the mothering she did not receive in one continuous relationship from one woman. A grandmother who held her. An aunt who saw her. A teacher who believed in her when no one in her own house did. An older friend in a season who taught her how a woman is supposed to be loved. A spiritual mother in a church who prayed for her with a tenderness no one had ever prayed over her with before.

Look back across your life right now and start to see them. The women God has sent. Some of them stayed for years, and some stayed for a season. Some you have lost touch with and some you still know. But each of them, in her time, was a piece of the mothering God was determined to give you, and you can see it now if you look back with the right eyes.

This is the pattern across the entire Word of God. Naomi mothered Ruth, who was not her daughter by birth, but who became her daughter by choice and by covenant. Elizabeth mothered Mary in the season Mary needed an older woman to confirm what God was doing in her body. The older women in Titus 2 are commanded to teach the younger women, because God designed the family of faith to be a place where women who had mothering to give would give it to women who needed mothering to receive.

You are not exempt from this design. You are part of it. The women God has been sending you, and the women God will yet send you, are part of the way He has been mothering you all along through other vessels when your first vessel could not pour what you needed.

And the day comes, in every healing daughter's life, when she is no longer only the one being mothered by these older women. She begins to mother younger women too. The very wound that ran through her childhood becomes the very thing that qualifies her to give to other women what she had to seek for herself. She knows what to look for, because she once needed it. She knows how to offer it, because she once received it from someone else. The wound becomes the doorway through which she gives away the mothering she had to learn to receive.

A note

For the daughter who became the mother.

Before we go further, there is a daughter reading this who became a mother herself, and she is carrying something specific that must be named.

You became the mother you did not have. You did it on purpose. You made an unspoken vow somewhere along the way that you would never do to your children what was done to you, and you have been quietly keeping that vow for years now, sometimes at great cost to yourself.

You are doing the holy work, and I want you to honor it. The work of breaking a generational pattern is some of the hardest spiritual work a woman ever does, because every instinct your nervous system was trained in has to be overridden by a different instinct, one you had to learn from scratch with no model to copy from. Every time you have given your children what you yourself did not receive, you have been rewriting an inheritance that was running through your family for generations.

But here is what I have to name for you, because no one else may have. The mothering you have been giving your children has been pouring out of a place that was never quite filled. You have been giving from your own ache. And while that has been beautiful for your children, it has been exhausting for you, and the exhaustion has been quietly building up over the years until you have started to wonder whether you have anything left in you at all.

This is why the work of this episode matters so deeply for you. Because the only way to mother sustainably out of plenty rather than out of ache is to let yourself be mothered too. By God. By the older women He has positioned in your life. By a vessel that is finally being filled, instead of constantly being drained.

You cannot keep pouring from a cup no one is filling. The cup needs to be filled. The Father who runs and the God who mothers has been waiting for you to bring your own cup to be filled, and not just to keep filling everyone else's. Today, the cup gets filled. Not tomorrow. Today.

Part 4

The cycle breaks here.

There is a teaching in the Word of God that is often misunderstood, and I want to address it here because it has terrified many a wounded daughter into thinking she has no hope of becoming a different kind of mother than the one she had.

"He punishes the children and their children for the sin of the parents to the third and fourth generation."

Exodus 34:7 NIV

The verse is true, and Scripture is honest about the reality that wounds and patterns and unholy inheritances do travel down through families. The patterns of how women mother often get passed mother to daughter to granddaughter for generations, and a woman can find herself, decades into her own life, suddenly doing the exact thing her mother did to her, the thing she swore she would never do, the thing she has been quietly horrified by for as long as she can remember. The inheritance is real.

But the inheritance is not your destiny.

Three or four generations down, the pattern can be broken, and the very same Bible that names the generational pattern also names the one who breaks it. The cycle breaks at the daughter who does the work, the daughter who lets God mother her so that she can mother differently, the daughter who lays down what her mother handed her and picks up what her Father is offering her instead.

You are that daughter. That is why you are here. The fact that you are reading this and not sleepwalking through the same patterns means God has been preparing you, possibly for years, to be the one in your family line who breaks the inheritance. Some of those women in your family line have been waiting for centuries for one of their daughters to finally do this work. You are the answer to the unspoken prayers of women in your line who never had the chance to be free themselves.

"Therefore, if anyone is in Christ, the new creation has come: The old has gone, the new is here!"

2 Corinthians 5:17 NIV

The old has gone. Whatever was being handed down to you that did not come from God does not have to keep traveling through you. In Christ, you are a new creation, and the pattern stops at your generation if you let God do this work in you. Your daughters will not inherit what you inherited. Your granddaughters will inherit a different woman than the one who came before. The line you stand at is not the end of an old pattern. It is the beginning of a new one.

That is the calling. To be the one who broke it.

Part 5

The discipline of honoring her anyway.

There is one more thing the Word of God asks of the daughter with a mother wound, and it is the hardest part of this entire teaching, but we cannot skip it because the principle is essential to the healing.

"Honor your father and your mother, so that you may live long in the land the Lord your God is giving you."

Exodus 20:12 NIV

The commandment is unconditional. It does not say honor your father and your mother if they have honored you first. It does not say honor your father and your mother provided they have earned that honor by the way they treated you. It simply says honor them, and it attaches a blessing to the daughter who does, and there is reason for the attachment of the blessing.

Honoring a mother who has wounded you is one of the most spiritually freeing acts a daughter ever does. Not because she deserves it. Often she does not. But because the act of honoring her, in spite of what she did or did not give, releases the daughter from a bondage she has been carrying without knowing it.

Unforgiveness binds the woman who refuses to release. The mother who hurt her may not even know she is being unforgiven, may not feel the weight of it, may go on with her life unaware. But the daughter who is carrying the unforgiveness is the one paying the price for it. Bitterness corrodes the vessel that holds it long before it reaches the one it was aimed at. And so the daughter, for her own sake, has to do the work of honoring even what was hard, of forgiving even what was unforgivable, of releasing even what should never have happened.

This does not mean pretending. It does not mean rewriting the story. It does not mean putting yourself back into harm's way if she is still actively harmful. Boundaries and honor are not mutually exclusive. A daughter can honor a mother from a distance, with prayer, with refusal to speak bitterness about her into the air, with a quiet refusal to repeat her patterns into the next generation. That is a kind of honor that protects the daughter and still obeys the commandment.

But the heart shift has to happen, because the daughter who refuses to make it stays bound to the very woman she is trying to free herself from. Forgiveness is the cutting of the cord. It does not require the other person's participation. It only requires your willingness, in the presence of God, to release what is no longer yours to carry.

And when you release it, something profoundly mysterious happens. The wound begins to heal at a deeper layer. Because forgiveness is not just emotional. It is spiritual. It is the act of agreeing with what God has already done in Christ, who forgave everything that was done against Him so that you could be free from the obligation to keep collecting debts against you. When you forgive your mother, you are walking in the very pattern Christ walked in, and the walking of it sets you free in a way that no therapy session, no journal entry, no inner work could ever set you free on its own.

Part 6

Three movements for this week.

And so we come to three movements to carry with us into this week. These three together are the beginning of letting God mother you in the very place where another mother could not.

Movement one

Name what was missed.

Take a quiet hour this week and write out, slowly and honestly, what your mother did not give you that a mother is meant to give. Maybe it was attentive presence. Maybe it was unconditional warmth. Maybe it was protection from the chaos in your home. Maybe it was the simple, daily, unspoken assurance that you were precious to her just for existing.

Do not generalize. Specific naming is what heals. The Psalmist named what he was lacking in language that did not soften the loss, and he brought it to God in its raw form, and God met him in the rawness, and the meeting was where the healing happened. Bring God exactly what was missing. Do not pretend it was not missing. Let Him see it. He has already seen it. He is just waiting for you to bring it to Him in your own words.

Movement two

Receive what He is offering in her place.

For every specific thing you named in the first movement, find a corresponding promise of God's own mother heart and receive it as the medicine for that specific ache.

If you named that your mother did not comfort you, receive that God comforts you as a mother comforts her child. Isaiah 66:13.

If you named that your mother forgot about you, receive that God will not forget you. Isaiah 49:15.

If you named that your mother never gathered you in, receive that Christ has longed to gather you to Himself the way a hen gathers her chicks. Matthew 23:37.

If you named that your mother was not safe, receive that God is your hiding place, that He shelters you under the shadow of His wings. Psalm 91.

The God of the universe has a specific verse and a specific promise for each specific lack in your story. The work is not to convince Him to give it. It is to receive what He has already declared Himself to be for you.

Movement three

Begin the work of release.

This week, write your mother a letter. Do not send it. The letter is not for her. It is for you. Write everything you have been carrying about her, every disappointment, every grief, every confusion, every quiet anger, every tender memory mixed with painful ones. Hold nothing back, because God already knows it all and the letter is being written in His presence.

And then, when you have written it, sit with God and ask Him to help you forgive her. Not in one moment. Not perfectly. Just begin. Forgiveness is rarely an event. It is most often a long and patient practice, with relapses and returns, with moments of release followed by waves of fresh grief. But the practice is the path, and the path leads somewhere real.

If she is still living, ask God to show you whether there is any practical step of honor He is asking you to take toward her. A call. A prayer. A boundary set with grace. Sometimes He asks for distance. Sometimes He asks for nearness. He will show you what fits your specific situation. The principle is the heart shift. The application varies, and He is the one who guides the application.

A brief note

On my own walk.

Dear woman, I will tell you the truth about my own walk through this wound, because I have lived inside this teaching, and the principles I have just laid before you are the principles I tested in my own life when nothing else worked.

My mother moved away from our home after she got remarried, when I was thirteen years old. I stayed behind with my brothers. The hurt cut so deep that I did not even speak to her for half a year, and when someone finally asked me whether I missed her, I had to think for a moment before I answered honestly that I did not. I had trained myself to stop thinking about her, because the noticing was more than I could carry at that age.

What you have to understand is that my mother is not a bad woman. She is one tough woman who survived more than most women survive in a lifetime. She was born in 1960 in a little village in Belarus to two parents who were extreme alcoholics, and she grew up in abuse and hard labor with no affection of any kind. She never heard the words “I love you” from her own parents, not once in her entire childhood. And so when she became a mother to twelve children, she could not give us what no one had ever given to her, because what she did not receive she did not have to pour out.

In 1995 my parents immigrated to the United States with their ten children, and I was born the next year, the eleventh child and the first one born on American soil. Not even two years after they arrived in this country, my father and four of my siblings were in a fatal car accident. My father, a brother and a sister died on the scene. Two of my other siblings survived in critical condition. My mother was left a widow with nine living children in a foreign country where she did not speak the language, and a few days before the funeral she discovered she was pregnant with her twelfth.

I cannot imagine the kind of grief she was carrying. And the pain she could not put down began coming out of her sideways onto her children, because hurt people hurt people, and the wound that has not been healed at the root will keep finding ways to express itself through the woman who is carrying it.

What I heard growing up was not “I love you.” What I heard was, “Go on out to the streets where you belong, you harlot.” What I heard was, “It would have been easier if I never had you.” What I heard was, “Why is God punishing me with you?” So when she moved away with her new husband when I was thirteen, the leaving was almost a relief from the daily verbal warfare, and at the same time it was a new and deeper wound, because the daughter who had been told she was a punishment now had the proof of it.

After she moved away, I fell into food addictions and eating disorders for five years, and then into depression, and then into suicide attempts. I was burying a pain so deep that I could no longer reach it, and the burying was killing me, slowly, in the way that buried things always do.

The shift began the year after high school, when I went to a ministry school in Pennsylvania. I knew that the work in front of me, if I was ever going to love myself, began with one impossible task. I had to forgive my mother. I did not know how. I did not know if I even could. But I made it my prayer for the year, and I made a quiet vow before God that whatever it took, I would do.

Then I made a wish I did not even think was possible. “God,” I prayed, “please let me hear the words ‘I love you’ from my mother at least once before I die.”

The first step was the scariest. I decided I would begin calling her once a month, and every time I made the call I would take a deep breath and whisper a prayer for strength before I dialed. For over six months, every single call went the same way. She would pick up. I would say hello and ask how she was. And then she would spend the next twenty to twenty-five minutes preaching at me, criticizing my way of life, admonishing my dress, telling me I needed to repent. I would take the phone off my ear and lay it on the table beside me, picking it up every minute or two just to say “mhmmm” so she knew I was still there. And every time, at the end of the call, before we hung up, I would tell her, “I love you.”

And for over six months, every single time, her response was the same. “I don’t need that kind of love.” And then she would hang up. And I would burst into tears.

But I had decided I would not give up. I started calling more often, weekly instead of monthly. I kept working on me, blessing on her, and praying for us. I was no longer waiting on God to change my mother while God was waiting on me to do the work that was mine to do. I was doing my part, and I was trusting Him to do His.

Then one day after about six months, she stopped saying it. The phrase that had cut me every single time simply disappeared from our calls. I told her I loved her, and she just hung up without commenting. That was the first miracle, and when I hung up that day, I cried for a different reason than I had been crying all the months before. Those tears were not pain anymore. Those tears were gratitude.

Three more months passed and the conversations began to transform. She stopped preaching and started telling me about her day. About the new calf that was born on her little farm. About the goat that was pregnant. About the chickens she had just bought. She began asking when I would come visit. She began telling me that life would be easier if I came to live with her, that I would not have to worry about food or housing if I were home with her.

And then on June 22, 2015, almost exactly a year from when I had started this work, she called me. I was on a lunch break in the park behind my office, and ten minutes into the conversation, in the middle of a sentence about something I cannot even remember now, she said it. “Dochinka, ya lublu tebya.” Daughter, I love you. In Russian. The words I had never heard from her in my entire life. The words I had begged God for, just a year earlier in ministry school, to let me hear at least once before I died. And I knew, the moment they came out of her mouth, that they had surprised her as much as they had surprised me, because I could hear her quickly change the subject as if she did not quite know where those words had come from. But I knew where they had come from. They had come from God, and they had come through her, and the prayer I had been praying that year had been answered in a park behind an office building during a lunch break on an ordinary Monday in June.

I muted the phone and I broke down. I had not been expecting it to come so soon. I had thought I might hear it from her deathbed, if I heard it at all. And here I was, hearing it from her lips while we were both alive, with years still ahead of us to begin again.

Today my mother and I have a better relationship than we have ever had before. The woman I once could not speak to is the woman I now call when she needs help with documents, because she still does not speak English. She gave me a car when I was working to become debt-free, and gift-giving happens to be my second love language. I make sure to fly out and visit her at least twice a year. I tell her I love her every single time we hang up.

What I learned in that year of calls is the principle I am giving you in this episode. You cannot change the mother who hurt you. You can only change yourself, bless on her, and pray for the both of you. And when you do, God does what only He can do, which is to soften a heart no human force could have softened, and to put words into a mouth that did not know how to form them, and to finish in her what He has already started finishing in you.

I am not telling you this work is fast. It took me a year of relentless calls before the first word changed. But the change came. And it came because the principles I have just laid before you are true, and they hold, and they will hold for you the way they held for me.

The mother who hurt me the most became my hero. The woman I could not speak to became the woman I cannot live without. And the wound I thought would define my life became the testimony I have been carrying ever since, the proof that the God who mothers when mothers cannot has never failed to be what He has named Himself.

Five questions for this week

Read one a day. Sit with it. Bring it to God in prayer.

One. What did your mother not give you that a mother is meant to give? Name it specifically, in the language you have been afraid to use even with yourself.

Two. How has that absence been translating itself into your relationship with love? Where have you been carrying the original ache into relationships that were never going to be able to heal it?

Three. Which women has God been sending across your life to mother you in pieces and in seasons? Have you been receiving them as the mothering God ordained, or have you been holding them at the distance you learned to keep with your own mother?

Four. Where is unforgiveness still sitting in you toward her, and what would it cost you to begin the practice of release? What might it free in you if you did?

Five. If you are mothering daughters of your own, what pattern is the Lord asking you to break in this generation that would have continued unbroken if you had not done the work?

A prayer for the daughter being mothered by God

Father, I come to You as the daughter I am, with the mother wound I have carried longer than I have even known I was carrying it.

I confess that I have been operating in this world out of a hunger I have not been able to satisfy through any human relationship. I confess that I have been pouring out of a cup that was never quite filled, and that I have grown tired in ways I have not been willing to name.

I bring You my mother. The woman she was. The woman she could not be. The places where she could not give me what You created me to receive. I lay her at Your feet, not in judgment, but in release, because I cannot keep carrying her in the place where I needed to be carried.

Be the mother I needed. Comfort me as a mother comforts her child. Remember me as the One who does not forget. Gather me to Yourself the way a hen gathers her chicks. Mother me, Father, so that I can stop trying to mother myself, and so that I can finally mother others out of plenty instead of out of ache.

Break the cycle in me. Let what was handed to me from generations of wounded women stop at my own life and not travel through me any further. Make me the daughter who broke the inheritance, and let the daughters who come after me inherit the freedom I have fought to receive.

In the name of Jesus, amen.

Before you go

One small permission.

This week, one practice. Give yourself, with no apology and no explanation to anyone else, the one form of mothering you wish you had received and never did. Not in a grand way. In a small one.

Maybe it is rest, because no one ever told you that you were allowed to rest. Maybe it is a quiet morning with a cup of tea and a book that has nothing to do with productivity. Maybe it is preparing yourself a meal with the same care you would prepare for someone you loved. Maybe it is putting on something soft and comfortable in the middle of the day for no reason at all. Maybe it is wrapping yourself in a blanket and letting yourself cry for ten minutes without anyone interrupting you and without trying to fix what is rising in you.

Whatever it is, give it to yourself this week. And as you do, recognize that you are not being indulgent. You are receiving what should have been freely given to you long ago and was not. You are practicing the receiving that you have been refusing to practice your whole life. The receiving is the work, and the work is healing you while you do it, even when you cannot feel the healing happening.

God has been mothering you all along. The practice this week is to finally let Him.
